Morning pages, created by Julia Cameron, are deceptively simple: three pages of longhand writing, first thing in the morning, every day. No rules about content. Just write.

What emerges is remarkable: a practice that clears mental clutter, unlocks creativity, and provides surprising self-insights.

## Stream of Consciousness

The key is not thinking about what to write. You're not journaling about meaningful experiences or crafting elegant prose. You're dumping whatever's in your head onto the page.

Worries about work. Frustration with traffic. Dreams you half-remember. Things you need to buy. Resentments you're holding. Creative ideas. Whatever flows, write it.

This stream-of-consciousness approach bypasses your inner critic and accesses thoughts you didn't know you were having.

## The Mental Drain

Your mind holds an enormous amount of background processing: worries, to-dos, resentments, half-formed ideas. This creates cognitive load that drains your attention all day.

Morning pages are a mental drain. You empty the cache. All those background processes get written out, freeing up mental RAM for what actually matters.

Many people report feeling clearer, lighter, and more focused after morning pages—like they've cleaned out mental clutter.

## Why Longhand?

Writing by hand is slower than typing, which means your thoughts move faster than your hand. This creates separation between thinking and writing. You can observe your thoughts rather than getting caught up in them.

Longhand also engages different brain networks than typing. There's something about the physical act of writing that accesses different modes of thinking.

## The Surprising Benefits

Beyond mental clarity, regular practitioners report:
- **Creative breakthroughs**: Ideas emerge that wouldn't surface otherwise
- **Self-knowledge**: Patterns become visible over time
- **Emotional processing**: Feelings get acknowledged and metabolized
- **Problem-solving**: Writing about stuck situations often reveals solutions
- **Reduced anxiety**: Worries lose power when externalized

You're not writing for anyone else. These aren't meant to be reread or shared. They're a process, not a product.
